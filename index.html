OverviewWhat can be confirmed from databases, literature and the PS2 analysis as of 2026-10-05

LACTBL1 encodes a 579-amino-acid protein built mostly on a beta-lactamase-like fold. Its RNA is highest in the retina, and single-cell data place it in retinal bipolar cells. Orthologs exist in every major vertebrate group, and the most distant one-to-one ortholog is in the tunicate, so the gene is at least 600 million years old. The only paralog in humans that can be recognized from sequence is the mitochondrial serine protease LACTB. The two share less than 20% identity and split before cnidarians and bilaterians did. Its rate of evolution is middling: about 3 times that of cytochrome c and half that of fibrinogen alpha. The most conserved residues cluster in the beta-lactamase catalytic core. No one has tested the activity of this protein yet.

    What was covered

  4. 9/21Mon#4Global sequence alignmentAlign the human protein end to end with the same protein from other species and see which positions never changed. Unchanged positions are usually the most important.Zoom · recorded

    What was covered

    • Similarity and homology are different things: similarity is a percentage; homology means two sequences share a common ancestor, and it is yes or no. Alignment asks how similar is similar enough to infer homology.
    • Alignment assumes the sequences changed step by step from a common ancestor: bases or amino acids were substituted, inserted or deleted.
    • Conserved positions tend to matter most for structure and function. But in very close species (e.g. rat and mouse) many positions simply have not had time to change, so more distant comparisons tell you more.
    • Homology does not mean the same function: one enzyme evolved into a structural protein in the eye lens.
    • A substitution matrix scores each substitution: identical rare amino acids score highest, chemically very different swaps are penalized most.
    • Global alignment suits single-domain proteins that have not diverged much; use local alignment for highly diverged, multi-domain ones.
    • Hands-on: find mouse, chicken, zebrafish and fruit fly homologs in NCBI Protein and align them to the human sequence with EMBOSS Needle.

    What I did

    • Ran Needle (EBLOSUM62) on human LACTBL1 (NP_001276903.1) vs mouse Lactbl1 (NP_001230191.1): identity 81.1%, similarity 86.4%, gaps 0.3%.
    • The most different stretch is aa 186–200, where mouse has an insertion. The S-x-x-K motif at S142–K145 is also present in mouse.

  5. 9/23Wed#5Dot plots: finding repeats within a sequencePlot a sequence against itself (or another sequence). Diagonal lines are similar segments; lines parallel to the main diagonal mean the sequence contains repeats.Due todayWT1Wikipedia training 123:59 · extra creditZoom · recorded

    What was covered

    • Local alignment only looks for short stretches of similarity, which suits modular proteins. Many proteins are built from several kinds of domains (examples: coagulation factor XII and tPA), and these modules spread through internal gene duplication and exon shuffling.
    • How to draw one: one sequence across, one down; set a window (how many residues compared at a time) and a threshold (minimum matches to plot a dot). A looser threshold gives more dots and more noise.
    • Reading it: in a self comparison the main diagonal is always there and means nothing; parallel lines next to it mean internal repeats.
    • Tools: Dotlet for protein, DNAdot for nucleic acid.

    What I did

    • LACTBL1 against itself (Dotlet, BLOSUM62, window 15): no parallel lines off the main diagonal, so no internal repeats.
    • A few scattered short dots are proline-rich segments that happen to look alike (e.g. aa 62–76 vs 447–461), not repeats.

  7. 9/28Mon#6BLAST: searching a whole database for similar sequencesBLAST is local alignment plus database search: it reports only the best-matching stretches, ranked by score, ignoring names. This class covered setting parameters, reading results and saving hits as FASTA.Due todayCQ2ChimeIn23:59 · 2 pts

    What was covered

    • Five kinds of BLAST: blastn, blastp, blastx, tblastn, tblastx. blastp is the first choice for finding homologs: the code is degenerate and DNA changes faster than amino acids, so distant relatives that are unrecognizable at the DNA level can still be recognized at the protein level.
    • Settings: database refseq_protein. Organism set to Homo sapiens alone finds paralogs; human plus the exclude box finds orthologs. The web page now defaults to ClusteredNR, which merges sequences over 90% identical into one entry and makes it easy to miss homologs, so switch it back.
    • Reading results: hits are ranked by score, not identity. Read identity together with Query cover: in the slides the orca hit has 81% identity but covers only 69%, so it ranks below a bat hit with 73% identity.
    • E value: how many hits this good you would expect by chance in a database this large. Smaller is more trustworthy, and the same alignment gets a larger E value in a larger database.
    • XP_ accessions and entries labeled PREDICTED are computer-predicted sequences. They are usable, but check for misjoins and missing ends.
    • Twilight zone (not covered separately in the slides; added from Rost 1999): at 20–35% identity, the number alone cannot tell you whether two sequences are homologous. The instructor's rule is to stop once identity drops below 25%.

    What I did

    • Collected 20 ortholog proteins, 21 with human, saved as FASTA: 3 mammals, 2 birds, 3 reptiles, 2 amphibians, 2 lobe-finned fish, 2 ray-finned fish, 2 cartilaginous fish, and 1 each for lamprey, sea anemone, sponge and tunicate. Human first, the rest sorted by identity from high to low; the lowest is 31.8%.
    • Checked the 4 most distant ones (lamprey, sea anemone, sponge, tunicate) separately: alignment scores against human LACTBL1 are 546–871, against the paralog LACTB only 54–149, so they belong to the LACTBL1 branch.
    • The paralog file is not done yet. LACTB and LACTBL1 share about 18–23% full-length identity, right in the twilight zone, so the reasoning has to be written out clearly.

  8. 9/30Wed#7BLAT: extending incomplete sequences at both endsSome mRNA or protein records are missing their ends. First use BLAST to find longer sequences in other species and in ESTs, then use BLAT to check them against the genome and fill in the missing piece.Workshop

    What was covered

    • Goal: extend the mRNA in the 5′ direction (same idea for 3′); the same approach can fill in a missing N or C terminus of a protein.
    • Method: BLAST the 5′UTR plus the first 5 codons, put the 5′UTRs of primate orthologs into one FASTA side by side, and see which extends furthest and which are cut short. The instructor demoed with SARAF and gained 10 bases from another sequence.
    • Then search the EST database (huge numbers of short, rough cDNA fragments), which added another 76 bp. Plus/Plus means no reverse complement is needed. With Plus/Minus, the apparent extra 428 bp is an artifact; the real gain is at most 120 bp, and it has to be confirmed by BLAT against the human genome.
    • Once the 5′ end is extended, check the Kozak sequence (CRNCATGR); it also roughly shows where transcription starts.
    • Protein example: the pangolin TMEM223 sequence only lines up from human position 34, so its N terminus is missing. BLAT of the human sequence to the pangolin genome hit a sequencing gap, so the only options are another species or another database. The instructor kept this slide on purpose as an example of "BLAT found nothing".

    What I did

    • BLATed all of NM_001289974.2 to hg38: all 1,950 nt matched (100%), chr1:22,953,043–22,972,866, minus strand, spanning 19,824 bp.
    • Listed the coordinates of the 8 exons and the lengths of the 7 introns (340–6,879 bp): exons 1,950 + introns 17,874, exactly 19,824. Exon 1 is followed by GT, consistent with the GT-AG rule.
    • Revised the English PS1 to version 4 and brought it to the Workshop.
    • Next step: this mRNA record has no 3′UTR (see Still to check), so I can use this class's method to look for ESTs in the 3′ direction.

EvolutionPS2 analysis, 10/2–10/4; methods and all numbers are in PS2 draft v33

LACTBL1 is an old gene. Every major vertebrate group has it, and tunicates have a one-to-one ortholog, so the chordate ancestor 600 million years ago already carried it. Its split from the paralog LACTB is even older: sea anemones have both, so the duplication happened before cnidarians and bilaterians separated (about 680 million years ago). Among bilaterians it was found only in chordates; the nematode and fruit fly branch lacks it.

Paralog LACTB

E = 6×10⁻⁸phmmer local hits on two beta-lactamase domain segments

Full-length global identity is only 15.9%, indistinguishable from shuffled-sequence controls (11.5 ± 4.6%); only HMMER picked it up. BLAST against all human RefSeq hits only LACTBL1 itself. GLS and GLS2 share only the fold, with no sequence hits; LACTB2 has a similar name but is a metallo-beta-lactamase, not a homolog.

Rate of evolution

0.62×10⁻⁹substitutions / site / year (fit on 16 jawed vertebrates)

Cytochrome c is 0.20 and fibrinogen alpha 1.39; LACTBL1 sits in between. m = −ln(1 − p), straight-line fit through the origin. All three mammals sit high, and so do both reference proteins, so the problem is with the mammal points, not LACTBL1 itself.

LACTB duplication time

≥ ~900 Myrrough estimate 0.92–1.01 Gya, usable only as a lower bound

Human LACTBL1 vs LACTB: p = 0.684, m = 1.15, divided by each gene's own rate. At p this high, the Poisson correction underestimates m. Consistent with the sea anemone having both genes: older than 680 million years.

Most conserved 5%

29 residues7 are invariant in all three alignments

S142, K145, T208, G218, Y256, S333, D337 are fully invariant. 28 of the 29 fall in the Pfam domain (87–435) and line up with the catalytic center of E. coli AmpC (S142-x-x-K145 ↔ Ser80/Lys83). None are in the N-terminal transmembrane segment. So it has kept the fold, and possibly the active site; the sequence cannot tell us the substrate.

Two unrooted trees

The gene tree (17 strict orthologs, NJ, 1,000 bootstraps) recovers 6 groups from the species tree with 95–100% support. Disagreements are all on short branches with support below 85%, with no sign of paralogs in the ortholog set. The tunicate gets pulled next to the mammals, probably long-branch attraction. The time-calibrated tree uses TimeTree to date 16 nodes.
